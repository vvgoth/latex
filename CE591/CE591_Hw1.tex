\section{Question 1}

The dataset has 23 velocity measurements from a steady boundary layer flow in the DTU tilting flume. The boundary conditions $u(0) = 0$ and $u(h) = U_0 = 0.3$ m/s are added to get 25 points total.

\noindent\textbf{(a)} Plot the mean velocity as a function of y using linearly scaled axes.

Figure~\ref{fig:q1a} shows that the velocity increases rapidly near the bed and then approaches the free-surface velocity more gradually. This shape is expected for a boundary layer flow because the bed creates strong shear near the wall, while the velocity gradient becomes smaller away from the bed.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{Q1a.png}
    \caption{\small Mean velocity profile on linearly scaled axes.}
    \label{fig:q1a}
\end{figure}
 
\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent\textbf{(b)} From the velocity profile in the previous item, calculate the depth averaged velocity.

The depth-averaged velocity was calculated from

\[
V = \frac{1}{h}\int_0^h u(y)\,dy
\]

Since the velocity measurements are not equally spaced in the vertical direction, the velocity profile was first interpolated using a spline function. Then the interpolated profile was integrated numerically in MATLAB. The calculated depth-averaged velocity is

\[
V = 0.2643~\text{m/s}
\]

This value is lower than the free-surface velocity of $0.3$ m/s, which is expected because the velocity decreases toward the bed due to the no-slip condition and bed shear.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{(c)} Estimate the friction velocity Uf from Darcy-Weisbach equation.

The friction velocity was first estimated using the Darcy-Weisbach equation. The cross-sectional area, wetted perimeter and hydraulic radius are calculated as

\[
A = hb = 0.021~\text{m}^2
\qquad
P = 2h+b = 0.44~\text{m}
\qquad
r_h = \frac{A}{P} = 0.0477~\text{m}
\]



Then, the Reynolds number and friction coefficient are

\[
Re = \frac{r_h V}{\nu} = 1.26 \times 10^4
\qquad
f = \frac{0.0557}{Re^{0.25}} = 0.0053
\]

Finally, the friction velocity is obtained as

\[
U_f = \sqrt{\frac{f}{2}}V
= \sqrt{\frac{0.0053}{2}}(0.2643)
= 0.0135~\text{m/s}
\]

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{(d)} Plot u versus y on a semi-log scale. Identify
the approximate lower and upper bounds of the logarithmic region. Compare Uf with the estimation made in part (c).

For the semi-logarithmic velocity profile, the vertical coordinate was converted to wall units as

\[
y^+ = \frac{U_f y}{\nu}
\]

using the friction velocity found in part (c). The lower limit of the logarithmic region was taken as $y^+ = 30$. The upper limit was taken as the top of the constant stress region, $y = 0.1h$. Therefore,

\[
y^+_{\text{upper}}
=
\frac{U_f(0.1h)}{\nu}
=
\frac{0.0135(0.1)(0.07)}{10^{-6}}
\approx 95
\]

Thus, the logarithmic region is approximately between

\[
30 \leq y^+ \leq 95
\]

A straight line was fitted to the velocity data in this range in the form

\[
u = m\ln(y^+) + B
\]

The fitted equation was obtained as

\[
u = 0.03298\ln(y^+) + 0.0670
\]

For the logarithmic law of the wall, the slope is related to the friction velocity by

\[
m = \frac{U_f}{\kappa}
\]

where $\kappa = 0.41$. Therefore, the friction velocity obtained directly from the fitted data is

\[
U_f = m\kappa = 0.03298(0.41) = 0.0135~\text{m/s}
\]

The friction velocity from part (c) was

\[
U_{f,c} = 0.01355~\text{m/s}
\]

while the value obtained from the logarithmic fit was

\[
U_{f,d} = 0.01352~\text{m/s}
\]

The percent difference is

\[
\frac{|U_{f,c}-U_{f,d}|}{U_{f,c}}\times100
=
\frac{|0.01355-0.01352|}{0.01355}\times100
=
0.20\%
\]

The two values are almost the same. This means that the Darcy-Weisbach estimate is consistent with the friction velocity obtained directly from the measured logarithmic velocity profile. The small difference is expected because the Darcy-Weisbach method uses an empirical friction coefficient, while the second method depends on the selected data points in the logarithmic layer.

\noindent\textbf{(e)} Using the $U_f$ from part (d) above re-plot the velocity profile in a dimensionless fashion. Identify the various regions of the flow.

The velocity profile was re-plotted in dimensionless form using the friction velocity obtained from part (d). The vertical coordinate and velocity were non-dimensionalized as

\[
y^+ = \frac{U_f y}{\nu}
\qquad
\frac{u}{U_f}
\]

The main flow regions were identified using approximate wall-unit limits. The viscous sublayer is taken as $y^+<5$, the buffer layer is taken as $5<y^+<30$, and the logarithmic layer starts after $y^+=30$. The upper limit of the logarithmic layer is taken as the top of the constant stress region, which is again around $y^+ \approx 95$.

In Figure~\ref{fig:q1e}, the first data points are close to the viscous sublayer and buffer layer. The measured velocity increases rapidly near the bed, showing the strong velocity gradient in this region. Between $y^+=30$ and $y^+\approx95$, the profile follows the logarithmic region. Above this range, the data belongs to the outer region, where the velocity continues to approach the free-surface value.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{(f)} Plot the van Driest velocity distribution the same diagram and compare the experimentally determined velocity profile with van Driest profile. 

The van Driest velocity distribution was plotted on the same graph as the dimensionless experimental velocity profile. The distribution was calculated from

\[
\frac{u}{U_f}
=
2\int_0^{y^+}
\frac{dy^+}
{1+\left[1+4\kappa^2(y^+)^2\left(1-\exp(-y^+/25)\right)^2\right]^{1/2}}
\]

The integral was evaluated numerically in MATLAB using \texttt{cumtrapz}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:q1e}, the van Driest distribution follows the experimental data reasonably well, especially through the buffer and logarithmic regions. Near the wall, the experimental points are slightly above the van Driest curve. This may be caused by the limited number of data points very close to the bed and by the sensitivity of the velocity gradient in the near-wall region.

Overall, the comparison shows that the measured profile is consistent with the expected turbulent boundary-layer behavior.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{(g)} Calculate the Reynolds stress. Plot the non-dimensional Reynolds stress as a function of y/h.

The Reynolds stress was calculated from the difference between the total shear stress and the viscous shear stress:

\[
-\rho \overline{u'v'} = \tau - \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
\]

where the total shear stress was taken as

\[
\tau = \rho U_f^2 \left(1-\frac{y}{h}\right)
\]

The velocity gradient was calculated using finite differences between consecutive velocity measurements. Therefore, the Reynolds stress values were obtained at the midpoints between the original $y$ locations. The final result was non-dimensionalized by $\rho U_f^2$ and plotted against $y/h$.

Figure~\ref{fig:q1g} shows that the Reynolds stress is small near the free surface because the total shear stress approaches zero as $y/h$ approaches 1. In the lower part of the flow, the Reynolds stress becomes larger because turbulent momentum exchange is stronger closer to the bed. The first near-bed point shows a sharp deviation because the velocity gradient is very large in the first interval, so the viscous shear term becomes dominant there.

\section{Question 2} Write a code that calculates the wave friction coefficients on both smooth and rough beds. Your code should also give estimations for the maximum friction velocity and boundary layer thickness for the given parameters based on the different flow regimes. 

\vspace{0.4cm}
A MATLAB function was written to calculate the wave friction coefficient $f_w$, maximum friction velocity $U_{fm}$, and boundary layer thickness $\delta$ for the given wave and bed conditions.

First, the wavelength $L$ was calculated from the linear wave dispersion relation. Then, the wave number and angular frequency were calculated as

\[
k = \frac{2\pi}{L}
\qquad
\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}
\]

The maximum near-bed orbital velocity and orbital semi-excursion were calculated as

\[
U_{0m} = \frac{H\omega}{2\sinh(kh)}
\qquad
a = \frac{U_{0m}}{\omega}
\]

The wave Reynolds number, relative roughness parameter, and equivalent sand roughness were calculated as

\[
Re_{\delta} = \frac{aU_{0m}}{\nu}
\qquad
\frac{a}{k_s}
\qquad
k_s = 2.5d_{50}
\]

These values were used to determine the flow regime. After selecting the correct regime, the corresponding expression for $f_w$ was used. Finally, the maximum friction velocity was calculated as

\[
U_{fm} = \sqrt{\frac{f_w}{2}}U_{0m}
\]

Case (a) is classified as a rough-bed case because the bed roughness is significant compared with the near-bed orbital motion. Because of this, it gives the larger wave friction coefficient. This means that a larger part of the wave energy is lost due to bed resistance.

Case (b) is classified as smooth turbulent. Although its friction coefficient is smaller, the maximum friction velocity is larger because the wave height and period are much larger, so the near-bed orbital velocity is much higher. The boundary layer thickness is also larger in this case due to the stronger orbital motion.

Overall, the rough-bed case is controlled more by bed roughness, while the smooth turbulent case is controlled more by the stronger wave motion. This is why the first case gives the larger $f_w$, but the second case gives the larger $U_{fm}$

function wave_friction(H, T, h, d50_mm)

nu  = 1e-6;
d50 = d50_mm * 1e-3;
k_s = 2.5 * d50;

L = dispersion_equation(T, h);
k = 2*pi/L;
omega = 2*pi/T;

U_0m = H*omega / (2*sinh(k*h));
a    = U_0m / omega;

Re_d = a * U_0m / nu;
a_ks = a / k_s;

fprintf('\n--- Input: H=%.3f m, T=%.1f s, h=%.2f m, d50=%.4f mm ---\n', ...
    H, T, h, d50_mm);
fprintf('L=%.4f m, U_0m=%.4f m/s, a=%.4f m\n', L, U_0m, a);
fprintf('Re_delta=%.3e, a/ks=%.2f\n', Re_d, a_ks);

if a_ks > 1e4   % Smooth bed
    if Re_d < 1.5e5
        f_w = 2 / sqrt(Re_d);
        regime = 'Smooth laminar';
        delta = (3*pi/4) * sqrt(2*nu/omega);
    elseif Re_d > 5e5
        f_w = 0.035 * Re_d^(-0.16);
        regime = 'Smooth turbulent';
        delta = 0.086 * a / Re_d^0.11;
    else
        f_w = 0.0045;
        regime = 'Smooth transitional';
        delta = 0.086 * a / Re_d^0.11;
    end
else            % Rough bed
    if a_ks > 50
        f_w = 0.04 * a_ks^(-0.25);
    elseif a_ks >= 10
        f_w = 0.4  * a_ks^(-0.75);
    elseif a_ks >= 0.2
        f_w = 0.32 * a_ks^(-0.8);
    else
        f_w = exp(5.5 * a_ks^(-0.16) - 6.7);
    end
    regime = 'Rough bed';
    delta = 0.09 * k_s * a_ks^0.82;
end

U_fm = sqrt(f_w/2) * U_0m;

fprintf('Regime: %s\n', regime);
fprintf('f_w  = %.4f\n', f_w);
fprintf('U_fm = %.4f m/s\n', U_fm);
fprintf('delta= %.4f m\n', delta);
end
